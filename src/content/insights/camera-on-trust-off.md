Most leadership teams solved the access problem years ago. Everyone can join the call. The harder problem is what happens once they have.

In my work with leadership teams, I hear the same quiet complaint in different words: "We talk all day, and I still don't know what my colleagues think." The calendar is full. The connection is thin.

## Presence is not the same as attention

A face on a screen signals attendance. It says very little about whether someone is listening, hesitating, or holding back a concern. Research on group communication has long suggested that people read subtle cues to judge whether it is safe to speak. Take those cues away and many people simply stay quiet.

That silence is easy to misread as agreement.

## Four changes that help

1. **Name the purpose in the first minute.** Is this meeting to decide, to discuss, or to inform? People contribute differently in each.
2. **Ask for a written thought first.** Give everyone two minutes to write a response before anyone speaks. It protects quieter voices and slows the loudest.
3. **Rotate who closes.** The person who summarizes the meeting learns to listen for what others meant.
4. **End with a one-line check.** "What is one thing that is still unclear?" takes thirty seconds and surfaces more than a long Q&A.

> Technology should widen the circle of people who can contribute, not narrow it to those who speak fastest.

## Take action this week

Pick one recurring meeting. Try the written-thought-first change just once. Notice who speaks that usually does not.

## The takeaway

Trust grows when people feel heard. Tools can carry the sound. Only habits can carry the meaning.
