When leaders say they want a more confident team, they often mean they want more confident individuals. That framing sends us looking for personalities. The better question is what the team practices together.

## Confidence is built in small, visible moments

Think about the last time a colleague admitted a mistake early. How did the room respond? That response taught everyone watching whether it is safe to do the same.

Over months, those small moments add up to a norm. Some teams learn that being wrong is costly. Others learn that being wrong early is useful.

## Three practices worth starting

### 1. Share the draft, not the polished version
Ask for early thinking in a shared space. The goal is to make unfinished work normal.

### 2. Close the loop on disagreement
When a person disagrees and the decision goes another way, explain why. People can accept a no. They struggle with silence.

### 3. Name what went well, specifically
General praise fades. "Your question in Tuesday's review changed our approach" is something a person can repeat to themselves.

## A note on evidence

The ideas here draw on a long line of research about psychological safety and group learning. They are consistent, but every team is different. Treat these as experiments, not rules.

## Take action

At your next team meeting, ask one person to share something that did not go to plan, and respond with curiosity. Then notice what happens the following week.
